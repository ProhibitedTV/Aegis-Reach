# AEGIS REACH // WORLD BIBLE

## The promise

Aegis Reach should feel like a place that existed before the player arrived and will continue to exist after they leave. The world must communicate history through geology, wear, construction logic, repeated human habits, and the consequences of old events.

The player should often understand *what happened* before anyone explains *why*.

## Where we are

### VESPER

Relayfall stands on **Vesper**, a cold tidally stressed moon orbiting the gas giant **Aurelia**.

Vesper is not a dead grey rock. It is a world in retreat.

For most of the colony's history the low basins held shallow, hypersaline surface seas fed by periodic cryovolcanic and geothermal upwelling. The current orbital cycle is colder and drier. Those seas have withdrawn into subterranean brine reservoirs, leaving enormous pale shelves of evaporite mineral, dark basalt terraces, salt-cut channels, and stranded industrial infrastructure.

Aurelia dominates the sky. Its gravity is not scenery: it drives Vesper's tides, stresses the crust, feeds geothermal activity, and determines when the old brine basins flood or recede.

### AEGIS REACH

"Aegis Reach" is not the moon's name. It is the colonial name for the remote defense/survey corridor along Vesper's Meridian Shelf: a chain of relay stations, extraction pads, geothermal plants, and orbital-defense uplinks spread across hundreds of kilometers.

**Relayfall** is one fortress in that chain.

That distinction matters. Seeing other beacons, ruined service routes, survey towers, and orbital infrastructure should make the player feel that the current level is a small part of a much larger frontier.

## The physical story of Vesper

### The old brine line

The single most important environmental storytelling rule on Vesper:

> Anything old enough and low enough should remember the sea.

Across cliffs, pylons, bunkers, retaining walls, and abandoned machinery there is a consistent historical flood elevation.

Below the old line:
- surfaces are pale from mineral deposition;
- paint is blistered or chemically stripped;
- metal is pitted and crusted;
- joints collect white salt fans;
- basalt is smoothed and stained blue-grey;
- shallow grooves show repeated brine flow.

Above the line:
- basalt remains dark and sharp;
- military paint survives longer;
- wind erosion dominates instead of salt abrasion;
- newer repair work visibly ignores the old color boundary.

No exposition is required. If the rule is consistent, players infer the vanished sea themselves.

### Tidal terraces

Open spaces should use broad readable terraces rather than random noise terrain. The moon has a geological rhythm:

1. dark basalt high shelf;
2. pale evaporite bench;
3. old brine channel;
4. another fractured basalt rise;
5. distant cliff or infrastructure silhouette.

These terraces create combat decisions as well as scenery: ridgelines, hull-down cover, flanking cuts, elevated firing positions, and open dangerous crossings.

### Tidal fractures

Aurelia's gravity repeatedly flexes Vesper. Long fractures run in families across the shelf. Human construction tends to bridge, reinforce, or avoid them.

Use fractures to explain:
- service bridges;
- geothermal vents;
- broken conduit runs;
- why a road bends;
- why a bunker is offset from another structure;
- why ancient/nonhuman geometry becomes exposed in later levels.

### The wind

The atmosphere is thin but persistent. Fine salt dust moves constantly across exposed shelves.

Visual consequences:
- leeward faces collect pale dust;
- door recesses remain darker;
- footprints/tracks can survive briefly in sheltered areas;
- cloth markers and antenna wires align with prevailing wind;
- abandoned equipment develops a directional weathering pattern.

## Human history without lore dumps

Vesper was not founded as a battlefield.

Before AEGIS, people came here to map geothermal reservoirs, maintain orbital navigation, and study the strange repeating structures detected below the old sea floor. The military defense network was layered onto civilian infrastructure later.

That history should remain visible.

### Civilian layer

Older facilities are practical and slightly improvised:
- survey numbers painted by hand;
- tide-height marks scratched into pylons;
- repaired cable bundles using mismatched clamps;
- local nicknames stenciled over formal designations;
- insulated work lights added where original architects never expected humans to stand for long;
- little shelters built around geothermal warm spots;
- personal maintenance charms, ribbons, photographs, cup hooks, scratched jokes.

### Military layer

AEGIS construction is newer, heavier, standardized, and indifferent to the old site:
- bastion walls interrupt old survey roads;
- firing positions are cut into salt benches;
- relay crowns sit on civilian foundations;
- armor plating covers pieces of older machinery instead of replacing them;
- cyan/amber military state lighting overlays faded civilian color.

### Occupation layer

The Iron Wardens did not build Relayfall. They seized it.

Their presence should read through temporary decisions:
- barricades face inward as well as outward;
- supply crates block routes that were once clear;
- maintenance panels are pried open to bypass locks;
- local signage is covered rather than removed;
- firing lines prioritize control of the relay chain;
- some civilian survival equipment has been consumed, stripped, or repurposed.

This tells the player something important: the Wardens are not defending their home. They are holding somebody else's infrastructure hostage.

## The buried mystery

Vesper's first survey teams found repeating geometric anomalies beneath the old brine floor. They are not ruins in the familiar archaeological sense. They behave more like an engineered substrate integrated into the moon's crust: conductive ribs, resonant cavities, and impossible regularity exposed where tidal fractures cut deeply enough.

Colonial engineers call the material **the Choir** because active AEGIS scans produce harmonic interference near it.

Do not explain its origin early.

Early game evidence:
- old survey poles form circles around apparently ordinary rock;
- relay interference is strongest near certain fractures;
- mineral deposits grow in unnaturally regular bands;
- abandoned civilian notes use inconsistent names for the same phenomenon;
- the Iron Wardens are excavating where military logic alone would not justify excavation.

The player's first emotional question should not be "what alien species made this?" It should be "why does everyone here seem to know something I don't?"

## Characters

### VANGUARD SEVEN — player

The player is a field operator, not a chosen-one demigod. Their power comes from competence, equipment, aggression, and the ability to understand a battlefield quickly.

Keep the player characterization restrained enough for projection, but establish one thing clearly: Seven has been to Aegis Reach before.

That allows tiny details to carry emotion. A destroyed survey shelter is not just scenery if Kestrel says nothing and Seven pauses near an old unit marking that matches their gear.

### KESTREL — mission controller / pilot

Kestrel is the player's human anchor. Dry, capable, concise. She knows the Reach and initially treats the mission as a technical recovery operation.

Her emotional arc begins when evidence shows the blackout was intentional and the Wardens are searching for something beneath the relay network.

Do not make her a lore narrator. Let her react to evidence the player has already seen.

### IRON WARDENS

The Wardens are a disciplined splinter force occupying the Reach. Their visual identity should remain human/military at first so the setting's stranger elements have room to escalate.

They believe control of AEGIS is necessary for survival in the outer colonies. Some of them may genuinely believe that surrendering the network to central command would doom settlements farther out.

That moral ambiguity should arrive later. In Relayfall they are still the people shooting at you.

### MIRA SEN — missing survey chief

A civilian geophysicist whose team mapped the Choir beneath Relayfall before the occupation. She never appears in a cheap hologram exposition dump.

Her presence is reconstructed from the world:
- survey marks;
- calibration tags;
- altered tide gauges;
- field shelters;
- one recurring hand-drawn waveform symbol;
- short technical voice fragments only when the player finds equipment that would logically contain them.

By the time the player hears her name spoken in dialogue, they should already recognize her work.

## Emotional rhythm

Aegis Reach cannot be wall-to-wall combat.

The level rhythm should alternate:

**wonder -> orientation -> pressure -> release -> curiosity -> pressure -> revelation -> escape**

Quiet spaces are not downtime to be filled with chatter. They let the player notice the world and make the next fight feel larger.

## Visual motifs

### Cyan
Active AEGIS systems, routes, navigation, restored human control.

### Amber
Industrial power, warning state, occupied/contested machinery.

### White mineral
Time, vanished water, exposure, history.

### Dark basalt
Vesper itself: old, heavy, fractured.

### The waveform
A subtle repeated shape associated with Mira's survey work and eventually the Choir. It should appear first as a believable scientific notation, not a magic glyph.

## Design rule

Every major environment asset must answer at least one question:

- What was this built for?
- Who used it before the fighting?
- What happened to it afterward?
- How did Vesper's climate/geology alter it?
- How does it affect the fight?

If an object answers none of those questions, it is probably clutter.
